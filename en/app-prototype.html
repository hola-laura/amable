App prototype: how to test your idea before paying to have it built

Building an app only to discover that nobody understands the main screen is the most expensive way to learn it. A prototype tells you in a week, when a change costs an afternoon rather than a budget.

In short
  • A prototype is a version you can touch but that does not yet work inside. It is for learning, not for launching.
  • There are three levels: paper (hours), linked screens (days) and a very small real first version (weeks).
  • You do not test whether people like it. You test whether someone manages to do what they came to do, without help.
  • Five people are enough to find most of the problems. Several small rounds are better than one big one.

We have seen it many times designing products: what seemed obvious in a meeting is not obvious in front of a real person. Nobody finds the button “you can’t miss”. And you do not discover that by giving opinions, but by watching someone use it.

The good news is that to watch it you do not need to have coded anything.

What is a prototype, and what isn’t it?

It is
  • The main screens, linked together
  • Something you can tap on a phone
  • Sample data that looks real
  • A tool for deciding
It is not
  • The finished app
  • Something that saves data or takes payment
  • A pretty picture to show off
  • A promise that it will be like this

What are the three levels?

Most ideas mainly need the second. It is the one that stops you building the wrong thing.

What exactly do you test?

You do not ask “do you like it?”. Almost everyone likes almost everything when you are the one showing it. You give a task and watch:

  • Do they understand what it is? In the first ten seconds, without you explaining.
  • Can they do the main thing? Book, order, log an expense. Whatever the heart of the app is.
  • Where do they stop? Every hesitation is a design problem, not the person’s.
  • What did they expect to find that is not there? That is where the truly missing features appear.

How many people do you need to test with?

Fewer than you imagine. Jakob Nielsen, of Nielsen Norman Group, explained it back in 2000: with about five people per round, most of the problems appear, because after that the observations repeat. His recommendation is to run several small rounds, fixing things in between, instead of one large study.

Five people, fix, another five. You learn more that way than with twenty at once.

One condition: they should be similar to whoever will use it. Your family does not count.

How do you run a test, step by step?

  1. Write three tasksAs a situation, not as instructions: “You want to move your Thursday appointment to Friday”. Not “tap My appointments”.
  2. Hand over the phone and stay quietAsk them to think aloud. Do not help, however hard it is.
  3. Note where they hesitateWhat they tap first, what they misread, what they look for and cannot find.
  4. Ask at the end“What did you expect to happen there?” teaches more than “what did you think?”.
  5. Group what repeatsWhat happens to three out of five is the first thing to fix.

Twenty minutes per person. In a week you have the round done and the changes clear.

What do you decide afterwards?

Go aheadThey manage itThe tasks get done and the flaws are small. Fix them and move on to building.
ChangeThey get stuckThe idea holds but the shape does not. Redo the prototype and test again. It is still cheap.
StopThey don’t need itThey understand the app but would not use it. This is the best moment to find out.

All three are good results. The bad one is not having asked.

What changes with AI?

Making the prototype is much faster. Tools such as Stitch or Figma Make produce linked screens from a description: we cover it in Google Stitch and Figma: what AI speeds up.

What does not change is the rest: deciding what to test, sitting down with five people and acting on what you see. And there is still some way from a prototype to a published app, which we explain in designing an app with AI isn’t as easy as it sounds.

Frequently asked questions

What is an app prototype?

It is a version of the app you can see and touch, with the main screens linked together, but which does not yet work inside. It lets you check whether the idea is understood and whether people can use it before it is coded.

How long does a prototype take?

It depends on the level: on paper, a few hours; with linked screens, a few days; a very small real first version, a few weeks.

How many people should you test a prototype with?

About five per round is usually enough to find most of the problems. Several small rounds, fixing things in between, are better than a single large one.

Can I make the prototype with AI?

Yes, and it is where AI helps most: generating screens quickly. What is still your job is deciding what to test, doing it with real people and changing what does not work.

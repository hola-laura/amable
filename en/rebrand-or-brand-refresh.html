Rebrand or brand refresh: how to tell which one your business needs

Before asking for “a new logo”, work out whether the problem is how you look or what you say. They are two different jobs, with two different prices.

In short
  • Refresh: the same brand, drawn better. It changes how you look, not who you are.
  • Rebrand: it changes what the brand promises, who it talks to and, sometimes, the name.
  • If the problem is visual, refresh. If your business, customer or price has changed, rebrand.

There is a moment almost every business owner recognises. You put your card next to a competitor’s and, without quite knowing why, yours looks like it’s from another decade. The first instinct is to ask for “a new logo”.

Sometimes that is exactly it. Other times, a new logo is like painting the front of a house with a leaking roof: it looks nice for a week and the problem is still inside. To know which one is yours, first separate two words people use as if they meant the same thing.

The difference in one sentence

A refresh keeps the brand and improves its form: the logo gets simpler, colours get organised and everything starts to look like one family. A rebrand changes the substance: what you promise, who you speak to and how you sound. Often the name changes too.

There is a third level in between, the touch-up: adjusting the logo so it reads small and fixing two colours and a typeface. It is the cheapest, and for many businesses it is what they actually need.

The test is no substitute for a conversation, but it gets the important part right: if you only tick image questions, you don’t need to reinvent yourself. And if you tick two or more business ones, no pretty logo is going to fix it.

When a refresh is enough

A refresh makes sense when people already know and like you, but your image doesn’t live up to it. It is the most common case for businesses that have been running for years: the logo was made in a hurry at opening, then came the website, Instagram, the shop sign and the invoices, and each one was solved on its own.

What it usually includes:

  • A simpler logo and a small version that reads in a 40-pixel avatar.
  • Two or three colours with exact codes, so the printer and the website show the same thing.
  • One typeface for headings and one for text.
  • Templates for what you use every week: posts, quotes, posters.
A good refresh shows in that nobody notices it: customers say “you look great”, not “did you change something?”.

When you need a rebrand

You need one when something fundamental has changed. You used to sell cheap and now you want to charge what you are worth. You did everything and now you specialise. Your name gets confused with another business, or nobody knows how to spell it on Google.

In those cases I don’t start with the logo. I start with the sentence you want people to repeat. When I designed Vereda, an olive oil brand, the challenge wasn’t making a pretty label: almost every premium oil already has one. The challenge was making it feel less like a gift for the cupboard and more like an oil you open on an ordinary Tuesday. Everything else came from that idea: the name, the hand drawings and the market colours.

What you shouldn’t change

A brand builds up value even when it doesn’t seem like it. Before throwing everything away, list what people already recognise:

  • The colour people know you by. If everyone talks about “the green shop”, the green stays.
  • Your Google reviews. If you change your name, update your Business Profile instead of creating a new one, or you will lose the reviews.
  • Your website. If the domain changes, old addresses must point to the new ones. Otherwise you will disappear from Google for a while.

How to change without losing customers

  1. List every place.Shop front, Google, Instagram, website, invoices, uniforms, vans. No brand change fails because of the logo; it fails because of the place someone forgot.
  2. Change what is most visible first.Google, social media and website on the same day. Printed material can wait until it runs out.
  3. Tell people.A rebrand with no explanation looks like a change of owner. One post and one sign are enough: what changes, what doesn’t and why.
  4. Give it three months.Anything new feels strange at first, to you too. Don’t judge it in the first week.

Questions

How often should a brand be renewed?

There is no fixed timeframe. A good brand lasts many years with small touch-ups. Review it when your business, customer or price changes, or when it no longer works where your customers are, such as on a phone.

Will I lose Google rankings if I change my name?

You may lose some for a while. To avoid it, update your Google Business Profile instead of creating a new one, redirect your website’s old addresses and change the name in the directories where you appear.

Can I change just the logo?

Yes, if the problem is purely visual. But a new logo with the old colours and fonts usually feels half done. At least review colours and typography at the same time.

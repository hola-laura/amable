UX audit for a service website: what to check when people visit but don’t get in touch

You have visitors, but the phone doesn’t ring. Before rebuilding the site or paying for ads, it’s worth reviewing it the way a first-time visitor would. This is the list I use, ordered by what matters most.

It’s one of the conversations I have most: “The website gets visits, but nobody gets in touch”. It’s almost never about the design being pretty or ugly. Somewhere along the way, the visitor hesitates, can’t find something or loses interest, and leaves.

A UX audit is exactly that: going through the site as a new customer and noting every point where they get stuck.

First, check your measurement

Before changing anything, ask yourself how you know nobody gets in touch. Many websites bring in customers who message on WhatsApp, call or book, and that doesn’t show up anywhere. If you only count forms, you may be seeing just part of the picture.

I explain it in how to measure if your website brings customers. With that sorted, you can start reviewing.

The five-second test

Open your site on a phone, look at it for five seconds and close it. Better still, ask someone who doesn’t know it. Then answer four questions:

1What do you do?In the customer’s words: “bathroom renovations”, not “integrated solutions”.
2Who for?Homeowners, businesses, landlords… People need to recognise themselves.
3Where?City or area. It’s the first filter for anyone looking for a service.
4How do I contact you?A clear button, without having to look for it.

If any of the four isn’t clear, that’s your first fix, before anything else.

Mobile and speed

Most people looking for a local service do it on their phone, and Google indexes websites based on their mobile version. That’s why the review is done phone in hand, not on a computer.

If your site is a long way from these figures, there are usually huge images, too many scripts or a very heavy website builder. It shows most on phones with an ordinary connection.

How to review it yourself in 30 minutes

  1. Pick one taskThe one that matters most: “ask for a quote” or “book an appointment”.
  2. Do it yourself on a phoneStarting from Google, not your homepage. Time it and note every hesitation.
  3. Ask someone elseSomeone who doesn’t know the site. Don’t help: watch where they stop.
  4. Go through the list aboveAnd note what you’re missing.
  5. Sort by impactFirst whatever stops people getting in touch; then what creates doubt; aesthetics last.

For a more methodical review, designers use Nielsen Norman Group’s ten usability heuristics: principles such as “always keep users informed about what’s going on” or “prevent errors rather than explain them”.

When to commission an audit

Doing it yourself is a great start. A commissioned audit is worth it when the site or product is bigger (several services, bookings, a customer area), when you’ve fixed the obvious and it still isn’t working, or when you need a prioritised list for your team.

Frequently asked questions

What is a website UX audit?

It’s a review of a website from the user’s point of view: you go through the important tasks (asking for information, booking, buying) and note where people hesitate, make mistakes or leave, ordered by priority.

Why does my website get visits but no enquiries?

Most often: it isn’t quickly clear what you do and where, there are no guide prices, examples or reviews, the contact button is hard to see or the form is long. First, check you’re also measuring WhatsApp, calls and bookings.

How fast should a website load?

Google considers a page good when its main content appears in 2.5 seconds or less, it responds to a tap in 200 milliseconds or less and it barely shifts while loading.

Can I do a UX audit myself?

Yes, as a start: try the main task on a phone, ask someone unfamiliar with the site to do it, and go through a list like the one in this article. For large sites or products, an expert review is worth it.

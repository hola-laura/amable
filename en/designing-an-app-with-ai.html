Designing an app with AI isn’t as easy as it sounds

AI writes the code for an app in seconds. What it doesn’t do for you is decide which app to build, how it works, what each button says and whether it really works. For that you have to wear a lot of hats.

In short
  • Yes, you can build an app with AI without being a developer. But “the AI made it” isn’t true: the AI writes, you decide.
  • Making an app means wearing eight hats: product, design, copy, code, testing, privacy, release and aftercare. AI only wears one.
  • The danger isn’t that AI fails. It’s that it hands you something that looks good in seconds. Your job is telling what looks right from what is right.
  • Use it to make things you couldn’t before, not to stop doing what you already know how to do.

A few days ago I read a designer explaining that he’d built an iPhone app from scratch in five days, with an AI coding assistant. The comments said what they always say: that he didn’t really build it, the machine did, and he just typed instructions.

I’ve been designing apps for years, first in large teams and now at amable, and I’ve published one of my own on the App Store. My short answer to that debate is that both sides get the same thing wrong: they think making an app means writing the code. It never did.

The short answer

Can you design and publish an app with AI without knowing how to code? Yes. AI writes the code, explains the errors and gets you out of plenty of dead ends. But the real work is everything else: deciding what the app does, designing every screen, writing every line of copy, testing it, looking after the data and publishing it. That is still yours.

Five days and a debate

The interesting part of that post wasn’t the five days. It was the list of things he had to learn along the way: using Git to save versions, finding his way around Xcode, designing the screens in Figma, writing down what the app should do, preparing scripts to test it and even building little tools to review every sentence. Breaking things, fixing them, changing his mind and carrying on. More than a hundred saved versions later, he said, it felt like the app was his.

He also admitted something I find the most honest part of all: he couldn’t vouch for the quality of the code underneath, but he could vouch for the product. For every detail, every decision about how it works and the hundreds of changes between the first version and the latest.

That’s the key. AI didn’t take the work away: it took a limit away. There were things he wanted to make that were beyond his technical skills or his time. Not any more. But the work stayed, and it changed shape: you no longer write every line, you do everything else at once.

The eight hats

In a team, several people make an app: someone in product, design, whoever writes the copy, engineering, testing, someone watching privacy. When you build it yourself with AI, those people don’t disappear. It’s you, wearing a different hat every hour.

1. Product: deciding what not to do

AI will build every feature you ask for, and that’s exactly the danger. Before you open it, write half a page on which problem the app solves, for whom and what it won’t do. That list of “no” saves you the most time later.

2. Design: the journey, not just the screens

If you don’t design, AI designs for you, with the usual result: an app that’s fine and interchangeable. Sketch the journey before the screens. And don’t forget the ones nobody draws: the empty screen on day one, the error screen, the loading screen, the offline one.

3. Copy: every word is a decision

An app has hundreds of small pieces of text. Each one can confuse or reassure. A trick that works: pull all the copy into a single list and read it in one go. That’s how you spot inconsistencies, the same button with three names or the error that blames the user.

4. Code: you don’t write it, but you have to understand it

You don’t need to know how to code, but you do need to learn just enough: saving versions with Git so you can go back, finding your way around Xcode, reading an error and knowing when the AI is going round in circles without fixing anything. That’s the moment to stop, undo and explain it another way.

5. Testing: “it’s fixed now” isn’t a test

AI will often tell you something works now. Always check. Write simple scripts (“I open the app offline”, “I turn on large text”, “I make a typo”) and repeat them after every important change. With TestFlight, Apple’s testing tool, you can invite up to 10,000 people to try it before you publish.

6. Privacy and security

If the app stores people’s data, you’re responsible for it. Apple asks you to declare what data the app collects before you publish and to have a privacy policy. And AI code isn’t always safe: a 2026 Veracode study found that, without security instructions, AI-generated code failed its security tests in a little over 40% of cases.

7. Release

To publish on the App Store you need an Apple developer account, which costs 99 US dollars a year, and you have to pass Apple’s review. You need screenshots, a description, keywords and a category. And the first time, they almost always send something back to fix.

8. Aftercare: an app is never finished

A new version of iOS comes out, someone finds a bug, a fair one-star review arrives. Publishing is the beginning. If you don’t have time to look after it, ask yourself whether you really need an app.

The danger: what looks right

AI makes it frighteningly easy to make bad things. You type one sentence and in a few seconds you get something plausible: clean screens, code that compiles, copy with a nice tone. It looks finished. And a lot of that reaches the public as it is.

AI doesn’t save you deciding. It saves you typing.

What separates a good app from one that only looks good is everything that happens after that first answer: the dozens of times you look at a screen and say “not this”, the button you move three pixels, the copy you rewrite because it sounded like a robot, the test you run on your mum’s phone. AI doesn’t do that. You do, or nobody does.

What it’s good for

I’m sceptical of a lot of what’s sold under the AI label too. I don’t want it writing my emails, or a chatbot answering my clients, or every product I use coming with an agent nobody asked for.

Where it does change my work is here: making things that used to be out of reach. I’ve always been a tinkerer, poking at a website’s code to see what happens. But some ideas stayed in a notebook because building them took a whole team. Not any more. I don’t want AI to do what I can already do. I want it to let me do what I couldn’t before.

What if you want an app for your business?

Before thinking about how to build it, ask whether you need it. Many businesses ask for an app when what they’re missing is a clear website or an online booking system, which your customers use without installing anything.

  • Build it yourself with AI if it’s for you or your team, if you can give it weeks and if you fancy learning. It’s the best way to understand how it all works.
  • Hire someone if your customers will use it, if it stores personal data or payments, or if your time is worth more in your business than learning Xcode.

Questions

Can you build an app with AI without knowing how to code?

Yes, but you’ll have to learn the basics: saving versions with Git, using Xcode for iPhone and understanding errors. AI writes the code; you decide what the app does, how it looks and whether it works.

How much does it cost to publish an app on the App Store?

An Apple developer account costs 99 US dollars a year. The app also has to pass Apple’s review, with screenshots, a description, privacy details and a privacy policy.

Is the code AI writes secure?

Not always. A 2026 Veracode study found that, without security instructions, AI-generated code failed its security tests in a little over 40% of cases. If the app stores personal data or payments, have someone who knows review it.

How long does it take to build an app with AI?

A first version can take days. Getting it right takes weeks, because almost all the time goes on deciding, testing and fixing, not on writing code.

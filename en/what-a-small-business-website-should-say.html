Web design for a small business: what the page has to explain to get enquiries

Whoever lands on your website arrives with five questions and little patience. If the page answers them in order, they write. If they have to hunt for them, they leave. Design helps; what convinces is what it says.

In short
  • A business website does not have to impress. It has to answer five questions, in this order: what you do, whether it is for me, why I should trust you, how much it costs and what I do next.
  • The first sentence says what you do and where, in the words a customer would use.
  • Trust is earned with things people can check: real photos, reviews with a link, specific projects.
  • A guide price (“from…”) filters and builds trust. And one main action, repeated, works better than five different buttons.

I have seen beautiful websites that do not get a single message and ugly ones that fill the diary. The difference is almost never the colours. It is whether the person who arrives finds what they came to ask.

This is what I write before designing anything. If you already have a website and want to know why it is not working, the list in what to check when people visit but don’t get in touch will serve you better.

Which five questions does a visitor bring?

  1. What do you do?In one sentence, without scrolling.
  2. Is it for me?My case, my area, my kind of problem.
  3. Can I trust you?Who you are and what you have done before.
  4. How much is it?At least roughly.
  5. What do I do now?One clear, easy step.

That is also the order of the page. Each section answers one.

1. How do you say what you do?

In the words your customer uses when searching, not the ones you use with colleagues:

AvoidBetter
“Complete solutions for your home”It could be anything.
“Bathroom and kitchen refits in Madrid”What and where. The same thing someone types into Google.
“Welcome to our website”Says nothing and takes the best spot.
“Physiotherapy with same-day appointments”What you do and the advantage that matters most.

It is also what Google needs to know when to show you. More on that in local SEO.

2. How does someone know it is for them?

Write your services as problems solved, not as a menu:

  • Instead of “Maintenance”: “Is the boiler noisy or not heating? We come within 24 hours”.
  • Instead of “Tax advice”: “We handle your self-employed taxes and warn you before each quarter”.

And say who it is not for. “We only work in the city” or “we don’t do new builds” saves time for both of you.

3. What makes someone trust you?

FacesReal photosYou, your team, your premises, your work. A stock photo shows.
Other customersReviews with a linkTwo or three, short, and a link to all the Google ones so they can be checked.
WorkOne specific projectWhat the problem was, what you did and what changed. One told well beats ten logos.

Never invented. If you have no reviews yet, start with how to get them and, meanwhile, show your work.

4. Should you show prices?

Almost always yes, even as a guide. A “from €800” does three things: it builds trust, it avoids enquiries that go nowhere and it saves you repeating the same thing in every message.

  • If the price is fixed: show it.
  • If it depends: “from…” and what makes it go up.
  • If it really cannot be given: explain how it is worked out and how long you take to send a quote.

This website does it that way: prices are published, with no small print.

5. What should the person do now?

One thing. Choose the one that suits you best (book, message on WhatsApp, ask for a quote) and repeat it in the same words at the top, in the middle and at the end.

Works
  • One main button, always the same
  • It says what happens: “Book an appointment”, “Ask for a quote”
  • Saying how long you take to reply
  • The phone number visible on mobile
Doesn’t work
  • Five different buttons competing
  • “Submit” or “More information”
  • A ten-field form
  • Hiding the contact details at the end of the menu

And make sure what arrives gets an answer: how to respond to website enquiries.

The page outline, to copy

What you can drop: the company history on the homepage, a photo carousel nobody waits to see, and the word “quality”.

Frequently asked questions

What should a small business homepage include?

A sentence saying what you do and where, one main button, your services explained as problems solved, real proof (reviews, photos, a project), prices at least as a guide, and a clear way to get in touch.

Is it a good idea to put prices on the website?

Almost always. A fixed price or a “from” builds trust and avoids enquiries that are not a fit. If you really cannot give a figure, explain what it depends on and how long you take to send a quote.

How many pages does a small business website need?

To begin with, a single well-ordered page is usually enough. Add pages when you have very different services that people search for separately.

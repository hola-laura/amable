Custom software or an off-the-shelf tool: how to decide what your business needs

I build custom software, and my first advice is still almost always: check whether it already exists. Here are the four steps I go through before proposing a tool of your own, and the signs that, this time, it’s worth it.

In short
  • Before choosing a tool, write the problem in one sentence: which task, who does it and what goes wrong today.
  • There are four steps: tidy up what you have, a tool that already exists, several connected tools and, last of all, custom software.
  • An existing tool is cheaper to start and ready today. Custom software fits how you work and doesn’t force you to change.
  • Custom is worth it when what exists makes you work worse, you pay for lots you don’t use, or the way you work is your advantage.

People often get in touch with their mind made up: “I want an app for…”. And often, after ten minutes’ talking, the answer is that they don’t need an app. They need to make better use of something they already have, or a tool that costs twenty euros a month.

Other times they do. The difference lies in the problem, not the tool.

Start with the problem, not the solution

Write down in one sentence what happens today. For example: “I do quotes at night, at home, copying prices from three different spreadsheets, and sometimes I get them wrong”. That sentence holds everything you need to decide:

The taskWriting quotesWhat exactly gets done, and how often.
Who and whereMe, at nightWho does it and in what conditions (on site, on a phone, in a hurry).
What goes wrongCopying and errorsThe specific pain. Any solution has to remove it.

The four steps

I always go through them in this order, and only move up when the previous step doesn’t remove the pain:

  1. Tidy up what you haveA template, a well-built spreadsheet, a folder with clear names. Free and sometimes enough.
  2. A tool that already existsBooking, invoicing, quoting or CRM software. If your trade has a good tool, start there.
  3. Several connected toolsWhat exists, joined up with automations so data isn’t copied by hand.
  4. Custom softwareA tool designed around how you work, when the previous steps leave you juggling.

What changes between an off-the-shelf tool and custom software?

Signs you need something custom

Probably yes
  • What exists makes you work worse than paper does
  • You use three tools and copy data between them every day
  • You pay per user for lots of features nobody uses
  • The way you work is exactly what sets you apart
  • You need it in a particular place: on site, in the van, at the counter
Probably not
  • There’s a tool in your trade that everyone uses and it works
  • You’re not yet sure how you want to work
  • The problem is organisation, not tools
  • You need it tomorrow
  • There’s no budget to maintain it

An example of mine: I built quoting software for a construction company. The tools available forced them back to the office to prepare each quote. The custom one lets them do it on site. It’s written up in quotes ready, without leaving the site.

The middle way

Between using something as it comes and building from scratch, there’s a lot of ground. What often works best is an existing tool for the common stuff (invoicing, booking) and a small custom piece for what makes you different, connected together.

Frequently asked questions

When is custom software worth it?

When existing tools make you work worse, you copy data between several programs every day or you pay for lots of features you don’t use, and you also know how you want to work and have budget to maintain it.

Is custom software cheaper than a subscription?

At first, the subscription is almost always cheaper. In the long run it depends on how many people use it, what you pay for features you don’t need and what it costs to maintain the custom tool.

Can I combine off-the-shelf tools and custom development?

Yes, and it’s often the most sensible option: an existing tool for the common stuff and a custom piece for what sets you apart, connected together.
